# Fatigue model on UTA-RLDD: what is demonstrated

Generated by `make model` (`data/pipeline.py`). Every number below is read from `data/out/metrics.json`; nothing is typed by hand, and the sentences that depend on a result are generated from it. It is not a medical device: the model estimates signs consistent with drowsiness, and the rules engine decides.

## In short

- **Data.** 72 videos of 24 people (12.2 h), three per person: alert, low vigilance, drowsy, as rated by the person. 3724 windows of 60 s (one every 10 s) are scored.
- **Validation.** leave-one-subject-out: every person is scored by a model that never saw them. All intervals are 95 % and resample people, not windows.
- **What ships.** Eye closure `blendshape`, features `absolute`: what the browser worker already computes, with no calibration step. It is a fixed setting decided by the team, not the winner of a comparison (see "The decision").
- **Demonstrated, feature by feature.** `perclos` alone separates alert from drowsy windows with AUC 0.763 (0.676 to 0.851); it is higher in the drowsy video than in the alert one for 21 of 24 people. `mean_blink_duration_s` alone separates alert from drowsy windows with AUC 0.733 (0.651 to 0.820); it is higher in the drowsy video than in the alert one for 18 of 24 people.
- **The model.** Three states: AUC **0.622** (0.574 to 0.675), macro F1 **0.427** (0.368 to 0.477), accuracy 44.1 % where chance is 33 %. Alert against drowsy, the `fatigueScore` reaches AUC **0.747** (0.672 to 0.826).
- **Against the browser's current formula.** On the same windows the formula gets 0.728 (0.646 to 0.817) alert against drowsy. Difference +0.019 (-0.033 to 0.074): the interval includes 0, so these 24 people do not show that the model beats the formula. On three states the difference in AUC is +0.023 (-0.015 to 0.064), which includes 0 too.
- **Calibration.** A per-person eye threshold (EAR) depends on where its calibration comes from: taken from the person's alert video it scores 0.804 alert against drowsy, taken from the scored video 0.666; `blendshape` without calibration scores 0.750 on the same windows. Features calibrated per person go from 0.811 to 0.553 the same way.
- **PVT.** Not measured: DROZY (video with PVT reaction times) is not downloaded, so the score has not been compared with any reaction time.

## What can be said

May be said:

- "Eye closure and blink duration predict drowsiness on 24 people the model never saw." Behind it: PERCLOS alone AUC 0.763 (0.676 to 0.851), blink duration alone 0.733 (0.651 to 0.820), the model 0.747 (0.672 to 0.826), alert against drowsy; every interval is above 0.5.
- "The camera estimates signs consistent with drowsiness; the rules engine decides."
- "Trained on volunteer drowsiness video with self-reported labels, not on astronauts."
- Any number of `data/out/metrics.json`, exactly as measured and with its interval.

May not be said:

- That the model beats the previous formula at telling alert from drowsy: the difference is +0.019 and its interval (-0.033 to 0.074) includes 0.
- That it tells the three states apart better than the previous formula: the difference is +0.023 (-0.015 to 0.064).
- That `yawns_per_min`, `head_pitch_std_deg`, `nods_per_min` add evidence: on their own their alert-vs-drowsy AUC is not distinguishable from 0.5 here. They stay in the model: features are not dropped after looking at results.
- That a personal calibration or the EAR method makes it better: RLDD cannot show it (see "What a rested calibration is worth").
- That the score equals alertness, performance or reaction time: the labels are the person's own rating and no PVT was measured.
- "Detects fatigue", "microsleep", a diagnosis, or anything about spaceflight: none of it was measured.

## The decision

Product decision of the team, taken after seeing the four-way comparison, the fold-internal selection and the calibration sensitivity reported here: ship eye closure `blendshape` with `absolute` features, which is what faceWorker.ts already computes and needs no calibration. Reasons: it does not depend on how a session starts, and it is the only variant whose evaluation does not depend on a calibration recording. It is a fixed setting of the pipeline, not a selection on test folds.

What that means for the numbers: no step of `make model` chooses the configuration, so the headline carries no winner's optimism. Among the four combinations that were measured, the shipped one ranks last on 3-state AUC (table under "Secondary analyses"). The decision was taken after seeing those analyses, so they are part of its history, not an independent confirmation.

## The data

UTA-RLDD (source `uta-rldd`), parts Fold1_part1, Fold1_part2, Fold2_part1, Fold2_part2: 24 of the dataset's 60 people. Phone and webcam recordings, 10.2 minutes each on average; 42 of the 72 are taller than wide. The label is the file name (0, 5, 10) and applies to the whole video.

- Frames are processed at 15 per second by the videos' own timestamps: 641068 frames in total. Processed rate per video: 11.4 to 15.1 per second (median 15.0); some recordings are slower than 15 fps or have a variable rate, and every duration below is measured in seconds, not in frames. Under 12 per second: Fold1_part1/06/10.mp4, Fold2_part1/18/5.mov.
- **No face** in 0.17 % of the frames (1058); per video, median 0.00 %, worst 5.00 %. No video is above 5 %. A frame with no face is missing, never a zero.
- **Rotation.** 29 videos carry a rotation flag; OpenCV applies it while decoding. The head roll measured on the decoded frames confirms it: the largest median roll of any video is 8.9 degrees, so no video is sideways.
- **Windows.** 60 s windows every 10 s from the first frame; used when they hold at least 450 frames and a face in at least 80% of them. Every window of the alert video that touches its first 120 s is left out, for training and for scoring: the calibrated alternatives use those seconds, and all four configurations are measured on the same windows. The shipped configuration does not use them for anything.

## The features and what each one shows

Seven numbers per window, defined in `data/pulsar_vision/fatigue_features.py` (version 1), which is the file the browser worker ports. All of them come from the MediaPipe FaceLandmarker output the browser already has, with the same model file. Eye closed: closed when (eyeBlinkLeft + eyeBlinkRight) / 2 > 0.5.

| # | feature | definition |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `perclos` | closed frames / frames with a face, in the window |
| 2 | `blink_rate_per_min` | eye closures shorter than 0.5 s, per observed minute |
| 3 | `mean_blink_duration_s` | mean duration of those short closures (last closed frame - first + one frame period); missing when there is none |
| 4 | `long_closure_rate_per_min` | eye closures of 0.5 s or more, per observed minute (assumption: the 0.5 s boundary) |
| 5 | `yawns_per_min` | yawns found in the window by the YawDD-calibrated detector (yawn_params.json), per window minute |
| 6 | `head_pitch_std_deg` | population standard deviation of the head pitch (degrees) over the frames with a face |
| 7 | `nods_per_min` | drops of the head at least 10 degrees below the window's median pitch (ending within 5), per observed minute (assumption: both levels) |

Clearly above 0.5 on these people: `perclos`, `blink_rate_per_min`, `mean_blink_duration_s`, `long_closure_rate_per_min`. Not distinguishable from 0.5: `yawns_per_min`, `head_pitch_std_deg`, `nods_per_min`. All seven stay in the model in this order: features are not pruned after looking at results.

Assumptions without a registered source: long closure from 0.5 s; a nod is a drop of 10 degrees that ends within 5; a window needs a face in 80 % of at least 450 frames. None of them was tuned against these results; nothing in the code can prove when they were chosen. PERCLOS is `fmcsa-1998-perclos`; the yawn detector is the one calibrated on `yawdd`, unchanged.

Checks on the real frames: the pitch grows when the chin goes down in 72 of 72 videos (median correlation with the nose position 0.92). The transformation matrix has `[0, 0, 0, 1]` as its last row in 640010 of 640010 frames with a face: row-major in Python, so `M[2][1]` here is `m[6]` in the browser's column-major array.

| alert | 249 | 691 | 107 | 23.8 % |
| low vigilance | 193 | 736 | 408 | 55.0 % |
| drowsy | 60 | 622 | 658 | 49.1 % |

Rows are the true state, columns the predicted one; counts of 60 s windows. Macro F1 0.427 (0.368 to 0.477), AUC 0.622 (0.574 to 0.675), accuracy 44.1 % (38.4 to 49.5 %), log loss 1.042 (a model that always says one third each scores 1.099).

**About the intervals.** Windows of a video overlap by 50 s and share one label: the window counts overstate the evidence. Intervals resample the subjects, not the windows. They are 95 % percentile intervals from 2000 resamples of the 24 subjects. They do not include the uncertainty of refitting the model.

`fatigueScore` (fatigueScore = 0.5 * P(low_vigilance) + 1.0 * P(drowsy)): mean 0.497 on alert windows, 0.532 on low vigilance, 0.588 on drowsy. AUC alert vs drowsy 0.747, alert vs the other two 0.676, drowsy vs the other two 0.692; Spearman correlation with the label 0.35.

**The score lives in a narrow band.** Out of fold it runs from 0.39 to 0.99; the middle half of the alert windows is 0.46 to 0.53, of the drowsy ones 0.50 to 0.65. A rested person does not score near 0: strong regularisation pulls every prediction towards the shares of the three states in the training windows, and two of the three are not alert. The browser's current formula sits near 0 for a rested face (mean 0.028 on alert windows, 0.067 on drowsy), so any threshold written for the old score does not carry over to this one.

Regularisation: C is chosen on the training subjects of each fold (grid under "How it was run"). It took the smallest value of the grid (the strongest shrinkage) in 24 of 24 folds. The grid was not extended after seeing this; a model that wants that much shrinkage is saying the features carry limited signal.

Whole videos: 32 of 72 right (44.4 %; a video gets the class most of its windows got; ties go to the class with the higher mean probability).

## The model the browser gets

`data/fatigue_model.json` is the shipped configuration refit on all 24 subjects (C = 0.001). The metrics above are the out-of-fold ones, not of this refit. Trained on: UTA-RLDD (Fold1_part1, Fold1_part2, Fold2_part1, Fold2_part2): 3724 windows of 60 s every 10 s from 72 videos of 24 subjects (1047 alert, 1337 low vigilance, 1340 drowsy; self-reported labels).

| intercept |  |  | -0.196 | 0.112 | 0.084 |

Weights apply to standardised features, so they compare across features: a positive weight raises that state's probability.

What the browser has to do, per processed frame at 15 fps and then every 10 s:

1. Keep 60 s of per-frame values: time, face found, eye closed, head pitch (`atan2(m[6], m[10])` of the transformation matrix), mouth opening ratio.
2. Eye closed: closed when (eyeBlinkLeft + eyeBlinkRight) / 2 > 0.5 (the same test as faceWorker.ts (BLINK = 0.5)).
3. Compute the seven features of the window with the definitions above (the unit tests in `data/tests/test_fatigue_features.py` are the examples to replay).
4. Skip the window if it has under 450 frames or a face in under 80 % of them: no score, not a zero.
5. Calibration: None. The score is available from the first usable 60 s window.
6. `z = (x - mean) / std`, with `z = 0` for a missing feature; `p = softmax(intercept + coef . z)`; classes in the order alert, low_vigilance, drowsy.
7. `fatigueScore = 0.5 * P(low_vigilance) + 1.0 * P(drowsy)`.

No EAR, no percentiles and no calibration are needed in the browser. They are documented in the feature module as evaluated alternatives only.

## Secondary analyses: the evidence behind the decision

Nothing in this section is the shipped model. These are the measurements the team saw before fixing the configuration; they stay in `make model` so the decision can be re-examined when better data arrives.

### Four combinations on the same windows

- Eye closure `blendshape`: mean of `eyeBlinkLeft` and `eyeBlinkRight` above 0.5; no calibration.
- Eye closure `ear_p80`: eye aspect ratio (`soukupova-2016-ear`) at or below `floor + 0.2 * (open - floor)`, i.e. at least 80 % closed (`fmcsa-1998-perclos`) relative to the person's own open eye, with open = P50 and floor = P1 of the first 120 s of their alert video.
- Features `absolute`: as computed. `calibrated`: minus, per person, the mean of each feature over the first 120 s of their alert video. **Both `calibrated` and `ear_p80` assume an alert calibration exists for each person.**

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `blendshape/absolute` **(shipped)** | 0.622 | 0.427 | 44.1 % | 1.042 | 0.747 |  | 24 of 24 |
| `blendshape/calibrated` | 0.666 | 0.461 | 47.3 % | 1.014 | 0.812 | +0.044 (0.013 to 0.070) | 24 of 24 |
| `ear_p80/absolute` | 0.676 | 0.505 | 50.3 % | 1.008 | 0.795 | +0.054 (0.014 to 0.091) | 9 of 24 |

On 3-state AUC `blendshape/calibrated`, `ear_p80/absolute` are ahead of the shipped combination by more than the noise between people. Every alternative takes something from the person's alert video (an eye threshold, a reference, or both); "What a rested calibration is worth" below measures how much rides on that.

### If the configuration were chosen by a score

Picking the best of the four by out-of-fold AUC would have named `ear_p80/absolute` (0.676) and that number would be optimistic. Choosing inside every fold from the training subjects alone picks `blendshape/calibrated` in 11, `ear_p80/absolute` in 11, `ear_p80/calibrated` in 2 folds and scores AUC 0.656, macro F1 0.465, alert-vs-drowsy AUC 0.781. The pick changes from fold to fold: these people do not single out one combination.

### What a rested calibration is worth

ear_p80 takes its eye threshold from the first 120 s of the person's ALERT video. What if it comes from the first 120 s of the video being scored, whatever state the person is in? The out-of-fold `ear_p80/absolute` models, unchanged (trained with the alert-video threshold); only the test subject's windows are recomputed with a threshold from each video's own first 120 s. Both versions are scored on the same windows: those that touch the first 120 s of no video.

The same question for the `calibrated` features: the out-of-fold `blendshape/calibrated` models, unchanged, with the test subject's reference taken from the first 120 s of each video instead of the alert video.

| `ear_p80`, threshold from the scored video | 0.583 | 0.405 | 0.666 | 2.99 % | 4.07 % | 7.44 % |
| `calibrated` features (blendshape), reference from the alert video | 0.667 | 0.458 | 0.811 |  |  |  |

Windows: 1047 / 1049 / 1052 (alert / low vigilance / drowsy). The browser's formula gets 0.738 alert vs drowsy on them. Alert-video threshold minus scored-video threshold: alert-vs-drowsy AUC 0.138 (0.037 to 0.226), 3-state AUC 0.101 (0.040 to 0.157). The same difference for the `calibrated` features: alert-vs-drowsy AUC 0.258 (0.126 to 0.394), 3-state AUC 0.139 (0.057 to 0.223).

RLDD has no recording that starts rested and then turns drowsy, so neither version is the product's case. The alert-video threshold assumes the calibration was taken rested; the scored-video threshold is what happens when the session starts with the person already in the state being measured.

**The EAR floor is often not a closed eye.** Across people the open level has median 0.294 and the floor 0.148; the floor is between 4 % and 75 % of the open level, and above half of it in 11 of 24 people. The likely reason (not checked frame by frame): two rested minutes at 15 frames per second hold too few fully closed frames for the 1st percentile to reach one. For those people the rule fires on a half-closed eye. A calibration that asks the person to close their eyes for a moment would measure the floor directly; that was not tested.

RLDD records the three states of a person as three separate videos, often on another day, device or camera angle. The EAR threshold and the calibration reference come from the alert video and are applied to the other two. The product calibrates at the start of the session it then monitors, a case RLDD does not contain; `calibration_sensitivity` measures what changes when the threshold comes from the scored recording itself.

The six EAR points of each eye sit where the formula assumes (lid points between the corners in 99.96 % / 99.95 % of the frames, right / left eye; upper lid above lower lid in 99.52 % / 99.40 %), so the indices are not the problem.

## Limits

- **Self-reported labels.** Each person rated their own state once for the whole video. Nobody measured their alertness, and a rating of 5 or 10 does not mean every minute of the video looks drowsy. This caps what any model can score here.
- **Not a controlled vigil.** One recording per state per person, on their own phone or webcam. Nothing shows how drowsy a person was beyond their own label. It is not a work shift and not a crew.
- **24 people.** These are 24 of the dataset's 60 (the parts downloaded so far). The intervals are wide because of it.
- **Correlated windows.** Thousands of windows come from a few dozen videos; a window count is not a sample size. The intervals resample people.
- **Three separate recordings per person**, often with another device, distance, light or camera angle. Part of what separates the states may be the recording, not the person. This applies to the shipped model too, not only to the calibrated alternatives.
- **Phone and laptop geometry.** 42 of the 72 videos are taller than wide, which a laptop webcam never is; the demo is a laptop webcam. Head pitch and the blendshapes change with the camera angle. Glasses and lighting change the eye measurements.
- **Frame rate.** At 15 frames per second a blink is a handful of frames: its duration moves in steps of about 67 ms and the shortest blinks can be missed. 10 videos could only be processed at under 13 frames per second, where the steps are longer.
- **The first 120 s of every alert video are left out** of training and scoring, because the alternatives calibrate on them; the other two videos keep theirs. The shipped model would not need that, and the three states therefore have unequal window counts (1047 / 1337 / 1340).
- **Assumptions.** The 0.5 s long-closure boundary, the nod levels and the window rule have no registered source. They were not tuned against these results.
- **Yawns** use a detector calibrated on acted yawns of drivers (YawDD). Nobody labelled yawns in RLDD, so its counts here are unchecked.
- **No reaction-time ground truth.** `pvt_correlation` is null until DROZY is available.
- **Not validated for spaceflight**, and head nods rely on gravity: in microgravity we expect the eye features to carry the signal (our hypothesis, no source).

## How it was run

- `make model` = `cd data && uv run python pipeline.py`. MediaPipe runs once per video and is cached in `data/features/rldd/` (git-ignored, coordinates and scores only: no frame or image is written anywhere). A second run reads the cache.
- FaceLandmarker `face_landmarker.task` (sha256 `64184e229b263107...`), the file the browser loads; VIDEO mode, one face, confidences 0.5.
- MediaPipe time: 31.7 ms per frame on average; decoding plus MediaPipe took 175 to 1354 s per video (median 567 s), 12.35 h summed over videos. Extraction runs: 5 videos in 5.8 min with 5 processes; 67 videos in 67.1 min with 11 processes. Per-frame time is FaceLandmarker.detect_for_video in Python on this machine's CPU, measured while several worker processes ran at once. It is not a browser measurement.
- Validation: leave-one-subject-out; C from [0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0, 100.0] by lowest pooled log loss over 5 subject-grouped folds of the training subjects; seed 2026. The scaler, C and the weights never see the test subject. The configuration is a constant of the pipeline (`SHIPPED`), not an output of it.
